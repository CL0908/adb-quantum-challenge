Information-Theoretic Reinforcement Learning for Joint Catastrophe Tails: Quantum Enhanced Rényi-Doob Policy Optimization

ADB Quantum Challenge — Joint-Loss Modelling for Natural Catastrophe Risk · Phase 1 Technical Proposal

Tan Chun Loong (Nanyang Technological University, Singapore)

November 2026

Summary

The problem. Pooled catastrophe bonds are priced and stress-tested on joint tails: how often several countries pay out, or are exhausted, together. For the ADB benchmark (Kyrgyz Republic + Tajikistan, earthquake + precipitation, 3-year term) the probability that both bonds are exhausted is ≈ 3×10⁻⁵. Monte Carlo returns exactly zero for it in 95–100% of runs at 1,024 paths, and exact enumeration grows as 10ⁿ with the number of countries. The bottleneck is accuracy per query in the rare joint tail, and how it scales with portfolio size.

Our approach. The zero-variance importance-sampling (IS) proposal is the Doob h-transform of the loss process. Equivalently, it is the optimal policy of a KL-regularised (soft) reinforcement learning problem. Rényi-Doob Policy Optimization (RDPO) learns a compact approximation of this policy by cross-entropy policy search. It then refines the policy with a Rényi-2 (χ²) policy gradient, because χ², not KL, is the divergence that controls IS variance. Q-RDPO carries the same change of measure into quantum amplitude estimation (QAE). Two observations make this work. First, payouts are capped, so the portfolio loss distribution loads exactly into a structured “Poisson-source” circuit with O(n + |E|) qubits and gates for any dependence graph. Second, exponentially twisting the source registers makes the importance weight bounded, so the QAE amplitude decays only with a large-deviation gap rather than with μ.

What we do not claim. There is no quantum advantage on the 2-country benchmark (exact enumeration takes 0.2 ms). We report ourselves that exact tensor networks solve sparse dependence graphs classically at any size. QAE needs early-fault-tolerant error rates (≤ 10⁻⁶ per two-qubit gate). A quantum wall-clock advantage is plausible only for large, densely dependent portfolios with very rare targets, and that is the regime the Deep Development phase is designed to test.

1. Problem and Approach

1.1 Framing the joint-loss problem

A portfolio covers countries i = 1…n with parametric payouts c_i(X) driven by a joint outcome X ~ p. Every quantity of interest is an expectation μ = E_p[f(c₁(X), …, c_n(X))]. In the ADB benchmark, earthquake payouts accrue in quarter steps of a USD 70m sub-limit (capped), precipitation pays a USD 10m sub-limit at most once, and dependence enters through shared events (common shocks) that hit both countries. We target the brief’s five quantities: expected loss, joint attachment, Pr(L > 80m), stop-loss E[(L − 80m)⁺] and the joint-exhaustion probability.

Two features define the difficulty:

  1. Rare joint tails. The decision-relevant quantities (joint exhaustion, exceedance of a pooled threshold) have probabilities of 10⁻³–10⁻¹³, and the dependence structure that drives them is exactly what historical data cannot constrain. Models must therefore be stress-tested across scenarios (the brief’s five scenarios), which multiplies the number of rare-event estimates.
  2. Portfolio scale. With two perils per country the annual state space is 10ⁿ; pooled facilities covering 5–30 countries are realistic for regional risk pools.

1.2 State of the art

Industry practice. Catastrophe models (vendor platforms and the open-source Oasis Loss Modelling Framework) chain hazard, vulnerability and financial modules. Portfolio metrics are obtained by aggregating simulated event or year loss tables, typically by plain Monte Carlo over 10⁴–10⁵ simulated years. Cross-country dependence enters through shared events or through copulas (Joe 2014). The known weakness is precision in the far joint tail at fixed simulation budget, which is precisely the brief’s bottleneck. Classical variance-reduction tools (stratification, quasi-Monte Carlo, importance sampling, the cross-entropy method; Glasserman 2003; Owen 1997; Rubinstein & Kroese 2004) help, but they need problem-specific proposal design, which is rarely done in production.

Quantum methods for risk. Quantum amplitude estimation offers a quadratic speedup in query complexity for Monte Carlo (Brassard et al. 2002; Montanaro 2015), with risk applications in Woerner & Egger (2019) and a correlated-default credit model (Egger et al. 2021), the closest published analogue to shared-event catastrophe risk. Practical variants avoid phase estimation (Suzuki et al. 2020; Grinko et al. 2021). End-to-end resource studies show that state preparation and arithmetic dominate costs and that advantage requires fault tolerance (Chakrabarti et al. 2021; Herman et al. 2023). Distribution loading by quantum generative models (QCBM, qGAN, QBM, quantum copulas; Benedetti et al. 2019; Zoufal et al. 2019; Amin et al. 2018; Zhu et al. 2022) is the standard near-term route, but these models are usually trained to reproduce p, which does not address rare-event variance. Tensor networks (Han et al. 2018; Orús 2014) are the main quantum-inspired classical alternative.

Gap we address. None of these works treats the change of measure (which distribution to sample or load) as the central learnable object, measures it with the divergence that actually controls variance, and carries it consistently into a quantum loader whose cost scales with the portfolio’s dependence graph.

1.3 Proposed approach

                 soft Bellman / Doob h-transform
 ADB model ──►  π*(y|s) ∝ p(y)·V(s⊕y)    (zero-variance IS policy)
                        │
          ┌─────────────┴──────────────┐
          ▼                            ▼
  RDPO (classical, runs today)    Q-RDPO (quantum estimation stage)
  log Ṽ_θ: O(n) features          exact Poisson-source loading circuit
  CE policy search (KL)           exponentially twisted source registers
  + Rényi-2 policy gradient (χ²)  → bounded weight → IQAE, O(1/√a) queries
          │                            │
          └──────► unbiased estimates of all portfolio targets ◄─────┘

Step 1: the zero-variance policy is a soft-RL policy. For a non-negative payoff f, the optimal IS proposal over the term is the Doob h-transform q*(y | s) ∝ p(y)·V(s ⊕ y), where V_t(s) = E[f | state s at time t] solves log V_t(s) = log Σ_y p(y) V_{t+1}(s ⊕ y). This is the soft Bellman equation of a KL-regularised control problem (Todorov 2009; Kappen 2005): log V is a soft value function and q* is its Boltzmann policy. On two countries V can be computed exactly (our strongest classical baseline). At scale it cannot, so we learn it.

Step 2: RDPO (classical, information-theoretic RL). We parametrise log Ṽ_θ(s) = Σ_i θ_i·ψ(a_i, r_i) + θ_g·g(L) with per-country threshold features and global loss features (O(n) parameters). We sample the portfolio source by source: each independent Poisson source has a finite capped-increment law with ≤ 25 values, so the policy’s normaliser is a sum over ≤ 25 values, never over 10ⁿ states. Training has two stages:

  • Cross-entropy policy search (minimises KL(p*‖q)): multilevel elite sets, convex updates.
  • Rényi-2 policy gradient (minimises χ²(p*‖q) = E_q[(wf)²]/μ² − 1, i.e. the IS variance itself): REINFORCE with reward −(w·f)². This step repairs CE fits that have small KL but heavy-tailed weights. Such policies exist, and we observed them.

The likelihood ratio keeps every estimate unbiased: a poorly learned policy only increases variance. A one-parameter exponential twist (the large-deviation Doob transform of total loss) gives a warm start.

Step 3: Q-RDPO (quantum estimation stage). Because each capped source has a finite increment law, the term distribution is loaded exactly by preparing one small register per source (3, 6 or 1 qubits), adding increments into per-country sum registers with in-place adders, and evaluating the payoff in an oracle. Twisting each source register, q_k(x) ∝ p_k(x)·exp(η ℓ_k(x)), changes only amplitudes, so loading costs nothing extra. The weight becomes a bounded function of total loss, so the amplitude estimated by QAE is a = μ·exp(ηT − Λ(η)) rather than μ.

Step 4: honest classical boundaries. We built the strongest classical competitors ourselves: zero-variance backward induction, and an exact tensor network (MPS) compressed in a tilted gauge so that tail targets keep relative precision. These define where quantum methods are and are not needed.

1.5 Feasibility on current and near-term hardware

  • Today (classical): RDPO runs on a laptop CPU. Fitting at 28 countries takes about 1 minute, and it is GPU-friendly (batched path sampling).
  • Near-term (NISQ): the 8-qubit QCBM proposal sampler has depth 9 and 32 CZ gates, which is feasible on current trapped-ion, neutral-atom and superconducting devices. We identified and solved the main practical issue: hardware noise biases IS weights by +9% to +60%, and calibrating the law that is actually sampled (post-readout) removes the bias to ≤ 0.6% with 10⁵ shots.
  • Early fault tolerance: QAE on the structured circuit needs ~50–600 logical qubits and 3×10³–1.2×10⁵ CNOTs per Grover iterate. Noisy maximum-likelihood AE beats MC only for two-qubit error ≤ 10⁻⁶ (break-even at 10⁻⁵). This is an early-fault-tolerant workload, not a NISQ one, and we say so.

1.6 Scalability and adaptability

  • Scaling. RDPO: O(n) parameters, O(n + |E|) cost per path; demonstrated to 28 countries. Q-RDPO: O(n + |E|) qubits and gates for any dependence graph, including dense ones, where the exact MPS bond dimension grows (χ = 58 → 135 from 4 to 6 fully connected countries).
  • Adaptability. The framework needs only (i) independent shock sources with finite (capped) effects and (ii) a payoff on aggregated states. This covers multi-peril parametric insurance, sovereign risk pools, correlated credit defaults with capped exposures (the Egger et al. 2021 structure), pandemic and supply-chain disruption bonds, compound climate events, and reliability of networked infrastructure (cascading failures).

2. Technical Rationale

2.1 Why these algorithms suit the task

Choice Reason
Doob h-transform as the target object It is the unique zero-variance proposal for non-negative payoffs, so approximating it is the principled route to variance reduction, not a heuristic tilt.
Soft-RL / policy parametrisation The h-transform is a soft-optimal policy, so RL machinery (value-function approximation, policy search) applies directly and scales through function approximation.
Cross-entropy, then Rényi-2 CE is robust for reaching rare levels (multilevel). Rényi-2 optimises the quantity that matters (variance) and repairs heavy-tailed fits: in our experiments the failure rate fell from 2/15 to 0/15 and the exact per-path std from 14.5 to 0.60.
Source-level sampling It replaces a 10ⁿ normaliser with ≤ 25-value normalisers and makes cost linear in sources. It is exact because capped increments compose: min(a + min(x,4), 4) = min(a + x, 4).
Structured (Poisson-source) loading It is exact with no truncation of Poisson counts, its gate count follows the dependence graph rather than 10ⁿ, and it parallelises across sources.
Exponential twisting inside the circuit It costs nothing to load and bounds the weight. This turns IS-inside-QAE from harmful (unbounded weights, a ≪ μ) into helpful (a = μ·exp(I(T))).
Iterative / maximum-likelihood AE These avoid quantum phase estimation (Grinko et al. 2021; Suzuki et al. 2020) and admit noise-aware likelihoods as error mitigation.
QCBM as near-term sampler It is shallow and hardware-native; with calibrated weights it is an unbiased near-term use of quantum sampling.

2.2 Benchmarks

Classical (all implemented): plain MC; scrambled Sobol (8 scrambles, t-interval); stratification by first payout year; the brief’s importance sampling; zero-variance backward induction (exact, 2 countries); cross-entropy IS (log-linear, factorised and pairwise); classical exponential twisting (Glasserman 2003); exact tilted-gauge MPS (to 28 countries).

Quantum (all implemented, exact simulation): QCBM proposal vs classical proposal families; generic Möttönen loading + IQAE; structured loading + IQAE; twisted structured loading + IQAE; noisy MLAE with and without noise-aware mitigation; variational quantum critic (hybrid quantum RL).

Reference values: the brief’s Table 5 (reproduced exactly), dense 10ⁿ enumeration (n ≤ 6) and the exact MPS.

3.4 Quantum stage

  • Structured loading is exact: a sparse-statevector simulation reproduces the full joint distribution to 10⁻¹⁶ and the ADB targets as QAE amplitudes.
  • Twisting reduces QAE calls at n = 28 from 2.8×10⁹ to 3.4×10⁴ (a = 8.8×10⁻⁴ instead of 1.3×10⁻¹³).
  • The QCBM proposal beats the brief’s IS (4–11×) and a factorized classical family, but not a log-linear classical family.

3.5 Classical boundary: where quantum is not needed

For chain, star and ring dependence the exact MPS bond dimension is constant (9 / 10 / 58), so all targets are computed exactly for 28 countries in under 70 s. For fully connected dependence it grows (58 → 135 from 4 to 6 countries). Compressing in a tilted (Doob) gauge is essential: plain SVD truncation incurs up to 2% error on the rarest target even at tolerance 10⁻¹². Bulk information measures (mutual information, entanglement ≤ 0.1 bit) do not predict the representation size that tail accuracy needs (χ ≈ 60).

3.6 Negative and mixed results we report

  • No quantum advantage at benchmark scale (exact enumeration: 0.2 ms).
  • The QCBM never beats a 15-parameter classical log-linear proposal.
  • State-dependent proposals inside QAE cut calls 54× but cost ~100× more per call to load.
  • The hybrid variational critic is unreliable on the rarest target. At constant depth it is also classically simulable.
  • QAE needs ≤ 10⁻⁶ two-qubit error to beat MC.

Ideal hardware modality. Dependence graphs translate into interactions between country registers through shared-source registers. Dense portfolios therefore need all-to-all connectivity: trapped ions or reconfigurable neutral atoms (parallel Rydberg gates also match the source-parallel preparation). On 2D superconducting lattices, SWAP routing inflates CNOT counts 10–40× (e.g. 3.2×10³ → 3.8×10⁴ per iterate for ADB). Chain and ring portfolios map to a line on any modality.

6. Commercialization and Impact

Where it fits in existing workflows. RDPO is a drop-in estimation engine. It sits between a catastrophe model’s event or source-level output (hazard × vulnerability × financial terms) and the portfolio metrics that pricing, structuring and monitoring teams consume:

  • Structuring parametric bonds and multi-country facilities: joint attachment and exhaustion probabilities, expected loss and stop-loss layers, computed precisely enough to compare pooling options and trigger designs across many scenarios.
  • Stress testing and model risk: fast, precise re-estimation of tail metrics under alternative dependence assumptions. This is exactly the brief’s scenario analysis, where data cannot pin down the dependence.
  • Regulatory and solvency tail metrics (e.g. 1-in-200-year losses) for insurers and reinsurers holding pooled sovereign risk.

Integration path. (i) An open Python library with a model-agnostic API (source laws + payoff in, unbiased estimates + error bars out). (ii) A plug-in for the open-source Oasis Loss Modelling Framework’s simulation stage. (iii) A hosted API for risk pools and development-bank teams. (iv) An optional quantum backend for the estimation stage as fault-tolerant hardware matures. The classical engine creates value immediately; the quantum stage is a forward-compatible upgrade path, not a dependency.

Potential users. Multilateral development banks’ disaster risk financing teams (structuring bonds such as ADB’s Kyrgyz–Tajik Disaster Relief Bond); regional sovereign risk pools (Caribbean, Pacific, African and Southeast Asian facilities); reinsurers and insurance-linked securities investors pricing pooled tails; catastrophe-model vendors and Oasis users; rating agencies and supervisors validating tail metrics.

Deployment considerations. Unbiasedness and reproducibility are built in (likelihood ratios, fixed seeds, exact reference checks), which supports model validation. The engine is CPU/GPU-light and transparent: the learned policy is a small set of interpretable feature weights. Main adoption barriers are integration with proprietary vendor models (mitigated by working at source / event level) and validation requirements (mitigated by exact benchmarks such as the tilted MPS on sparse portfolios).

Broader applicability. The same structure (independent shocks with capped effects, rare joint outcomes) appears in correlated credit portfolios, pandemic and parametric health bonds, supply-chain disruption insurance, compound climate extremes (heat–drought–flood), power-grid and infrastructure reliability, and operational risk. RDPO’s information-theoretic training (Rényi-2 policy gradients) is generic to rare-event simulation; Q-RDPO’s structured loading applies wherever a distribution is a product of independent local sources.

Impact. Better joint-tail precision lowers model uncertainty loadings in pricing, so pooled cover becomes cheaper for vulnerable countries. It also makes trigger and pooling decisions more defensible, which supports faster post-disaster liquidity (UN SDG 13).

  • Hardware / simulator: 4-core Intel Xeon @ 2.10 GHz, 15 GB RAM, CPU only. All quantum circuits are simulated by simulators written for this project: an exact real-amplitude statevector simulator (QCBM, variational critic), an exact sparse-statevector simulator (structured and twisted loading), and an exact IQAE/MLAE emulator from ideal or depolarised measurement statistics. No quantum SDK or hardware was used.
  • Reproducibility: every experiment uses fixed seeds recorded in its output; one command per phase (README); full pipeline ≈ 3 h on the hardware above.
  • AI use: the code, experiments and documents were developed with Claude Code (Anthropic) as an AI pair-programmer and writing assistant, following the author’s research plan. The author directed the research questions and design choices and reviewed the results; all numbers come from the scripts in the repository.
